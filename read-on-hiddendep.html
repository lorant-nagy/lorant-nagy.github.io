Hidden dependence and stochastic volatility

The coin-and-oracle game gives a small but memorable warning: a probabilistic structure may look completely innocent when inspected pair by pair, yet still contain a rigid hidden relation. Something similar can happen in time series. A process may look almost like pure noise through the lens of ordinary autocorrelation, even though a persistent latent mechanism is quietly at work.

The point is not that autocorrelation is wrong. The point is that it answers only one very specific question. If dependence survives in amplitude rather than in sign, then the usual product moment can fail to detect it.

1. The stochastic volatility paradigm

The basic stochastic-volatility paradigm separates returns into a fresh shock and a latent scale. After centering returns and suppressing drift terms for exposition, the essential structure is

\[ r_n = \sigma_n\,\xi_n, \qquad \xi_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1). \]

This already captures the key idea: signs are generated by fresh noise, while amplitudes are governed by a hidden volatility process.

In continuous time, classical stochastic-volatility models such as Hull and White (1987) describe the price \(S_t\) through a latent volatility process driven by a Brownian motion. A simple prototype takes the form

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma_t S_t\,dW_t^{(1)}, \]

where \(\sigma_t\) is itself a stochastic process. In these classical models the volatility has no long memory — dependence on the volatility side decays on short timescales.

Comte and Renault (1998) extend this picture by introducing a fractional driver on the volatility side, producing long-range persistence in volatility while preserving the stochastic-volatility structure. The covariance of the fractional Gaussian noise increments decays at rate \(|k|^{2H-2}\), which for \(H > \tfrac{1}{2}\) is not summable — the hallmark of long memory. This persistence can then appear in squared returns and other volatility proxies.

2. Isolating the mechanism

To isolate the masking phenomenon, we make three simplifications. First, set \(\mu = 0\) — returns have zero drift. Second, suppress deterministic drift on the volatility side as well. Third, work in discrete time with unit step. The return equation then reduces to

\[ r_n = \sigma_n\,\xi_n, \qquad \xi_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1), \]

with the only remaining content being that \((\sigma_n)\) is a persistent positive process. Persistence can now survive on the volatility side even when the signed returns themselves appear nearly structureless.

From this point on, we deliberately suppress all further features that are not essential for the masking phenomenon, and retain only the multiplicative structure of returns with a persistent latent scale.

3. A stripped-down discrete toy model

For maximum transparency, we simplify the volatility side one step further. Rather than working with a positive long-memory process, we take a fractional Gaussian noise sequence \((G_n)\) with Hurst exponent \(H \in (\tfrac{3}{4}, 1)\) and let it serve directly as the hidden Gaussian scale driver. We restrict to \(H > \tfrac{3}{4}\) because only in this range does the squared process \(X_n^2\) inherit genuine long memory rather than mere dependence. The observed series is then defined by

\[ X_n = G_n\,\varepsilon_n, \]

where \(\varepsilon_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) are fresh shocks, independent of \(G_n\). This is not the literal discretisation of the continuous-time model. It is a toy surrogate designed to preserve the key masking mechanism in the clearest possible form: a persistent scale process hiding behind rapidly changing signs.

4. The toy model — what autocorrelation sees and misses

With \(G_n \sim \mathrm{fGn}(H)\) as the hidden driver, the raw observations \(X_n\) inherit a changing sign from the Gaussian noises while their local scale is governed by \(G_n\). At the level of ordinary autocovariance, for distinct lags,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X_n, X_{n+h}) = 0 \qquad (h \neq 0), \]

because every cross-product contains at least one centered innovation with mean zero. The raw series has no nontrivial linear autocorrelation.

But now square the process:

\[ X_n^2 = G_n^2\,\varepsilon_n^2. \]

For \(h \neq 0\),

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X_n^2,\,X_{n+h}^2) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_n^2]\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{n+h}^2]\, \operatorname{Cov}(G_n^2, G_{n+h}^2) = \operatorname{Cov}(G_n^2, G_{n+h}^2). \]

The hidden dependence has not disappeared. It has migrated from the signed process into its magnitudes and squares.

5. Why the warning matters

In both the continuous-time model and the discrete toy version, the alternating signs of the shocks conceal the persistent mechanism that drives the scale. Ordinary autocorrelation answers only a narrow question: it checks for linear dependence in the signed process. It does not rule out dependence in amplitudes, squares, or local variance.

Zero autocorrelation is not the same as absence of dependence. The product moment is not false — it is simply too coarse to see the structure that matters here.

When volatility is governed by a long-memory driver, the alternating signs of returns can mask the underlying persistence. Ordinary autocorrelation may then suggest a world of independent shocks, while persistence quietly survives in volatility, return magnitudes, or squared returns.
