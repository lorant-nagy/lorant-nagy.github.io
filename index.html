A note on transient price impact

Trading moves the market against the trader, but what is the temporal nature of this effect? In price impact models, large traders are often modeled as generating permanent impact, whereas small traders are typically modeled as having only instantaneous impact. In reality, however, the truth likely lies somewhere in between. Price impact may be better described as transient: after a trade moves the price, the effect gradually decays, with the price reverting toward its original level over time.

Hidden dependence and stochastic volatility: when autocorrelation sees only noise

Take two fair coins, and add a third character: an oracle that reports only one thing — whether the two coins landed on the same face or on different faces. At first sight, the three objects seem perfectly unrelated: whichever pair we pick, one does not reveal anything about the other. But this is deceptive: independence holds at the level of pairs, yet the moment any two are known, the third is fixed with certainty. This somewhat paradoxical example is often cited to show that full independence in a probabilistic structure is a subtle property.

One may then naturally ask whether a similar hiding of dependence can emerge in more complex and evolving structures such as time series.

The answer is yes: in finance, one important example appears in stochastic-volatility models, beginning with Hull and White (1987) and continuing, in a long-memory direction, with the work of Comte and Renault (1998). When volatility is governed by a long-memory driver, the alternating signs of returns can mask the underlying persistence. Ordinary autocorrelation may then suggest a world of independent shocks, while persistence quietly survives in return magnitudes, local energy, or squared values. In this sense, what looks like noise at the level of returns may still carry memory through volatility clustering.
